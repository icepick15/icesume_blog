"Japa" — the now-common term for Nigerians relocating abroad — is usually planned around the visa: which country, which route (study, work, skilled migration), which documents. The financial side gets far less attention, and it's often where people either get stuck at the application stage or land abroad seriously underprepared.

This guide covers the money side of Japa specifically: what to actually save, how to hold and move it, and what happens to your Nigerian finances after you leave.

---

## Step 1: Understand That Your Budget Has Three Separate Phases

Treating "Japa costs" as one lump number is the first mistake. Break it into three distinct phases, because each has a different funding need and timeline:

**1. Pre-departure costs** — exam fees (IELTS/TOEFL where required), application fees, document processing, visa fees, medical/police reports.

**2. Proof of funds (POF)** — the amount immigration authorities require you to show you have, usually held for a minimum period before applying. This is the single largest number in most Japa budgets.

**3. Settling-in costs** — flights, initial accommodation deposit (often 1-3 months' rent upfront), transportation, first weeks of living costs before you have local income.

Trying to fund all three from one pot at the last minute is how people either fail the POF requirement or arrive abroad with almost nothing left.

---

## Step 2: Know Your Destination's Proof of Funds Requirement

Proof of funds requirements vary significantly by country and visa route, and they change periodically — always confirm the current figure directly from the official immigration source before applying, since these numbers move.

**General ranges to plan around (confirm current figures officially):**
- **Canada (study permits)** — beyond tuition, a set living-cost amount is required, historically shown as a single applicant figure that's higher for Quebec-bound students
- **UK (student visas)** — a monthly living-cost figure multiplied by the length of your course (higher for London-based institutions)
- **Skilled worker/work-permit routes** — POF requirements are often lower or replaced by an employer-sponsorship guarantee, but settling-in savings are still strongly advisable

**Where to check current figures:**
- Canada: [ircc.canada.ca](https://www.ircc.canada.ca)
- UK: [gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk)
- USA: [travel.state.gov](https://travel.state.gov)

Never rely on a blog post (including this one) for the exact current POF figure — immigration authorities update these, sometimes yearly.

## Step 3: Save in the Right Currency, in the Right Account

**Domiciliary accounts.** Nigerian banks offer domiciliary accounts that hold USD, GBP, or EUR directly. If your target country's currency is one of these, saving directly in that currency protects you from naira depreciation eroding your progress between now and your travel date.

**Why this matters:** if you save in naira and convert right before travel, months of savings can lose real value to exchange rate movement. Saving in the target currency from the start avoids this risk entirely.

**Where to open one:** most commercial banks in Nigeria (GTBank, Zenith, Access, UBA, and others) offer domiciliary accounts — requirements are similar to opening a regular account, with your BVN and valid ID.

## Step 4: Build a Realistic Savings Target and Timeline

A workable rule of thumb: your total Japa budget (across all three phases above) should be treated as **3-5x your visa's stated minimum proof of funds** once you add pre-departure costs and settling-in costs on top. Underestimating is the single most common financial mistake in the Japa process.

**Practical ways to build this fund faster:**
- Open a dedicated savings account or fixed-income investment separate from your everyday spending account, so it isn't accidentally spent
- Consider dollar-denominated investment apps for savings you won't need for 6+ months, since they combine currency protection with a return (see our guide on [best investment apps for Nigerians](/blog/best-investment-apps-for-nigerians-2026))
- If side income is part of your plan, dollar-paying remote work or freelancing can accelerate a foreign-currency fund directly (see [how to get paid in dollars from Nigeria](/blog/how-to-get-paid-in-dollars-from-nigeria))

## Step 5: Understand What Happens to Your Nigerian Tax Status

A common source of confusion: does Nigeria still tax you once you've relocated?

**General principle:** Nigeria generally taxes only Nigerian-source income — rent from Nigerian property, dividends from Nigerian companies, and profit from a Nigerian-based business. Salary earned abroad, from a foreign employer, for work performed abroad, is generally not taxed by Nigeria.

**Residency threshold:** you typically become a non-resident for Nigerian tax purposes once you spend fewer than 183 days in Nigeria within a 12-month period.

**Before you leave, practically:**
- File your final tax return as a resident for the year you depart
- Inform your employer's HR/payroll that you're leaving, so your final PAYE is settled correctly
- Keep records — proof of your departure date and foreign employment can matter if your tax status is ever queried

This is general guidance, not a substitute for advice from a tax professional familiar with your specific situation — cross-border tax questions get complicated quickly, especially if you retain Nigerian income sources.

## Step 6: Decide What to Do With Your Nigerian Financial Life

You don't need to close everything down. Common practice among Nigerians who've relocated:

- **Keep at least one active Nigerian bank account** — useful for receiving naira payments, supporting family, and managing any Nigerian subscriptions or obligations
- **Keep your pension (RSA) as is** — it stays yours and continues to exist even without new contributions; see our guide on [understanding your pension in Nigeria](/blog/understanding-pension-in-nigeria)
- **Review any Nigerian investments** (stocks, real estate) and decide whether to hold, liquidate, or manage them remotely
- **Set up a plan for sending money back**, if applicable — compare remittance platforms' fees and exchange rates rather than defaulting to whichever app is most advertised

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Underestimating settling-in costs** — the first month abroad, before any local income arrives, is often more expensive than people plan for.
- **Saving in naira until the last minute**, then losing value to a poor exchange rate at conversion time.
- **Not confirming current proof-of-funds figures** directly from the official immigration source before relying on a number from social media or an old blog post.
- **Ignoring the tax "exit" steps** — leaving without informing your employer or filing a final return can create avoidable complications later.
- **Treating Japa as a one-time cost** rather than a multi-phase budget with different timelines for each part.
