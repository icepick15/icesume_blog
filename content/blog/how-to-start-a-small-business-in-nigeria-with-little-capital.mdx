"How to start a business" was one of the most-searched phrases by Nigerians in 2026 — and for good reason. With formal employment hard to come by and the naira under constant pressure, more Nigerians are looking to build something of their own.

The good news: you don't need ₦5 million or a bank loan to start. Most successful small businesses in Nigeria today started with under ₦200,000 — sometimes far less. What matters more than capital is choosing the right business model, keeping your costs low, and registering properly from day one.

This guide walks through realistic low-capital business ideas, how to fund them without falling into predatory lending, and the legal steps to make it official.

---

## Step 1: Pick a Business Model That Matches Your Capital

Before picking an "idea," separate businesses into two categories based on what they actually require:

**Service businesses (lowest capital, fastest to start):**
- Freelance/digital services (writing, design, social media management, virtual assistance)
- Skilled trade services (makeup artistry, tailoring, phone repair, hairdressing)
- Consulting in an area you already have expertise in

**Product/trading businesses (require some inventory capital):**
- Reselling (thrift/"okrika" clothing, phone accessories, skincare products)
- Food and snacks (small chops, baking, food delivery)
- Agriculture-adjacent (poultry, vegetable farming on a small plot)

If your capital is genuinely under ₦100,000, start with a service business. You're selling your time and skill, not tying up cash in stock that might not sell.

## Realistic Low-Capital Business Ideas and What They Actually Cost

| Business | Estimated Starting Capital | What It Covers |
|---|---|---|
| Social media management / virtual assistant | ₦0 – ₦30,000 | Laptop (if you don't have one, this rises sharply), data, basic tools |
| Phone accessories/thrift reselling | ₦30,000 – ₦100,000 | Initial stock from Computer Village, Alaba, or wholesale markets |
| Small chops / baking from home | ₦20,000 – ₦80,000 | Ingredients, packaging, basic equipment |
| Makeup artistry | ₦50,000 – ₦250,000 | Starter kit (can start smaller with a basic kit and grow) |
| Dropshipping/reselling via Instagram/WhatsApp | ₦10,000 – ₦50,000 | Marketing, sample products, delivery logistics |
| Poultry (small scale, 20-50 birds) | ₦80,000 – ₦200,000 | Day-old chicks, feed, basic housing |

These are starting ranges, not fixed prices — costs vary by state and by how lean you're willing to run at the start.

## Step 2: Fund It Without a Loan Shark

Most Nigerians starting out don't have investor money. Here's the realistic funding order, from safest to riskiest:

**1. Personal savings.** Slower, but you own 100% of the business and owe nobody.

**2. Ajo/Esusu (rotating savings groups).** A common and low-risk way to build a lump sum with people you trust, without interest.

**3. Cooperative society loans.** If you belong to a workplace or community cooperative, their loans are typically far cheaper than commercial bank or app-based loans.

**4. Government-backed funding.** Genuine, no-collateral-required options exist but are competitive and require patience:
- **Bank of Industry (BOI)** — offers loans specifically targeted at MSMEs, often at single-digit interest rates
- **NIRSAL Microfinance Bank** — agriculture and MSME-focused lending
- **SMEDAN (Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria)** — training, and periodic grant/support schemes for registered small businesses

**5. Avoid:** high-interest instant loan apps for business capital. Their repayment terms (often 14-30 days with steep interest) can kill a business before it earns its first real profit. These are best avoided for business funding entirely.

## Step 3: Register the Business Early — Not "When It Grows"

A common mistake is running unregistered for years "until the business is big enough." This actually costs more in the long run — you can't open a business bank account, can't bid for any formal contract, and CAC name-availability issues get worse the longer a similar name exists unregistered.

Business Name registration with the **Corporate Affairs Commission (CAC)** costs roughly ₦10,000, is done entirely online, and takes about a week. For the full step-by-step process, see our guide: **[How to Register a Business in Nigeria: CAC Step-by-Step Guide](/blog/how-to-register-business-in-nigeria-cac)**.

## Step 4: Keep Business and Personal Money Separate From Day One

This is the single most common reason small Nigerian businesses fail to grow past year one. If your business's money lives in the same account as your rent and feeding money, you cannot tell whether the business is actually profitable.

- Open a separate account (even a free digital bank account) the day you register
- Track every naira in and out — a simple notebook or spreadsheet is enough at the start
- Pay yourself a fixed "salary" from the business rather than withdrawing whenever you need cash

---

## Step 5: Reinvest Before You Scale Your Lifestyle

The businesses that survive past the first year are almost always the ones where the founder reinvested profit into stock, tools, or marketing before increasing personal spending. Set a rule: for the first 6-12 months, a fixed percentage of profit (e.g. 30-50%) goes back into the business before anything is treated as personal income.

---

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Waiting for "enough" capital before starting.** Most viable low-capital businesses can start smaller than you think — start with what you have and grow.
- **Copying a business without checking local demand.** What works in Lagos may not work in your specific neighbourhood or town.
- **Ignoring registration until a customer asks for an invoice or a bank asks for CAC documents.**
- **Mixing business and personal finances**, which makes it impossible to know if the business is actually making money.
- **Taking high-interest loans to cover working capital** before the business has a proven, repeatable sales cycle.
